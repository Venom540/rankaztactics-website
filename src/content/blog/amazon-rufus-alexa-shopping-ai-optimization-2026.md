If you've been researching "Amazon Rufus" and noticed the name barely shows up anymore, you didn't miss anything — Amazon renamed it. On May 13, 2026, Rufus was folded into a new assistant called **Alexa for Shopping**, which combines Rufus's original product knowledge with Alexa+'s personalization layer. The underlying system, the data it reads, and the way it evaluates your listing are unchanged. If you still see "Rufus" somewhere in your account or in a screenshot from a guide, it's the same thing under an older label.

## What it actually is

Rufus launched in 2024 as Amazon's generative AI shopping assistant. Instead of typing "running shoes flat feet," a shopper can now ask something like "what running shoes are good for flat feet under $100," and the assistant interprets the intent behind that question rather than matching it to keyword strings.

By Amazon's own disclosures, the assistant reached over 300 million active customers in 2025 and was involved in roughly 38% of shopping sessions by Black Friday of that year, driving an estimated $12 billion in incremental annualized sales. This is not a side experiment — it's a core part of how Amazon wants people shopping now.

Under the hood, it runs on something called **COSMO**, a commonsense knowledge graph that maps products to use cases, audiences, and buying context rather than just keyword strings. Worth knowing: COSMO is built mostly from shopping behavior — what people search for and buy together — not by parsing your listing word for word. Your listing content is still the raw material the relevance models draw on, it's just not a direct keyword match the way traditional search is.

## How it's different from a normal Amazon search

Traditional Amazon search (still handling the large majority of discovery traffic, somewhere around 80% as of early 2026) is a keyword-matching system. The assistant reads your entire listing as a set of claims — your title, bullets, description, A+ Content, reviews, and Q&A — and cross-checks those claims against what your actual reviews say before deciding whether to recommend you.

A few places this shows up on the page itself: an "Ask" button on the product page that feeds your listing straight into a chat; a "Why you might like this" personalized blurb that appears on some detail pages; and a "Compare with similar items" option that opens the assistant directly from the cart. None of these are hidden features — they're already live touchpoints in the shopping flow.

## What actually helps

Keyword stuffing doesn't work here — natural language does. The assistant is trained to reward listings that read like they're answering a real question, and penalize ones that read like they were built for a search engine instead of a person. In practice that means:

**Fill in complete structured attributes.** Material, use case, certifications, compatibility, whatever's relevant to your category. Products with complete attribute data consistently outperform products that only have a keyword-optimized title and thin everything else.

**Answer the questions shoppers are actually asking, explicitly.** Look at your negative reviews and your Q&A section for recurring questions ("is this dishwasher safe," "will this fit a standard backpack") and make sure the answer is stated plainly somewhere in your bullets or A+ Content — not just implied.

**Test it yourself.** Literally open the assistant and ask it specific questions about your own product, the way a customer would. If the answer it gives back is wrong, thin, or missing, that's a direct signal about what to fix in your listing.

**Check Brand Analytics for early attribution signals.** Amazon has started exposing some AI-driven attribution data for Brand Registered sellers. A sudden shift in impression share on longer, conversational search terms is often a sign this channel is already sending you traffic.

## The honest scale of it, right now

Traditional keyword search is still doing most of the work — this isn't a case for throwing out your existing SEO strategy. But the share mediated by the assistant has been climbing steadily every quarter, and query behavior when it is involved looks noticeably different: questions run roughly twice as long and far more conversational than a typical search box query.

The sellers who get ahead of this now are treating it the way smart sellers treated mobile optimization a decade ago: not urgent yet for most categories, but clearly not going away either, and cheap to start preparing for today.

Review your top ASINs quarterly for this specifically, and do a lighter spot check monthly on whatever's driving the most revenue. The listings that already read like they're written for a person asking a genuine question tend to do fine here without a rebuild. The ones built purely around keyword density are the ones with real work to do.
