Starting July 27, 2026, Amazon is capping product titles at 75 characters — including spaces — in every category except media. That's down from as much as 200 characters in most categories today. If you haven't touched your titles yet, this is the one listing change worth prioritizing this month over everything else.

## What's actually changing

Two things are happening at once, and they work together:

**The 75-character limit.** Titles over the limit after July 27 will gradually be rewritten by Amazon's own AI, using whatever's already in your bullets, backend terms, and description. You don't get a say unless you act first.

**A new Item Highlights field.** This gives you an extra 125 searchable characters for the details that no longer fit in the title — materials, use cases, comparison points. It's indexed separately from your title and shows up alongside it in search results and on the product page.

Together that's 200 characters of visible, searchable space, just split across two fields instead of packed into one. The limit applies to both parent and child variation titles, and it follows an earlier tightening from January 2025, so this has been building for a while — it's just becoming a hard, enforced cap now.

## What happens if you do nothing

After July 27, Amazon's AI starts rewriting non-compliant titles on its own. If you're enrolled in Brand Registry, you get a 14-day window through the Review Listing Changes tool to review, edit, or approve each rewrite before it locks in. If you miss that window — or you're not brand registered — the AI's version just goes live.

The rewrites are grammatically fine and hit the character count, but they're pulled from existing content without knowing which specific keyword is actually driving your sales. There's a real chance your best-converting phrase gets dropped in favor of something more generic.

## One thing worth understanding about Item Highlights

Amazon indexes your title and your Item Highlights field separately. That means repeating the same keyword in both wastes character space — you're not gaining any additional indexing by saying it twice. Some agencies running their own tests report that Item Highlights may carry its own search-ranking weight, though Amazon hasn't confirmed that officially. What is confirmed: it's searchable, it's visible to shoppers, and an empty Item Highlights field is wasted real estate either way.

## A simple way to triage your keywords into 75 characters

Pull your Search Term Report and sort by attributed sales, not impressions. Most listings have two or three terms that actually drive the bulk of revenue — those are your **Tier 1** terms and they get the title. Everything else relevant but secondary, your **Tier 2** terms, moves into Item Highlights and your bullet points. Anything important that still doesn't fit anywhere visible can go into your backend search terms — just don't repeat what's already in the title or Item Highlights there either.

## A note on timing

If you're reading this before July 27, you're inside the safe window to make changes deliberately rather than reactively. A few practical rules for doing it well:

- Lead with brand, product type, and your single strongest differentiator in the **first 60 characters** — that's what actually displays on a mobile search result.
- Count characters with a formula (`=LEN()` in Sheets or Excel) rather than guessing; spaces and punctuation all count.
- Don't bulk-edit your whole catalog in one afternoon. Roll changes out in waves of 50 to 100 listings, keep a small control group untouched, and track click-through rate and conversion for two weeks before touching the rest.
- Watch for Amazon's tightened language rules alongside this: promotional words like "best," "cheap," "sale," "free shipping," and "guarantee" are being flagged or stripped from titles — this is a good moment to clean those out if they're still in there.

The sellers who come out of this well aren't the ones who just shaved characters off their existing titles. They're the ones who used the forced rewrite to build a cleaner, more deliberate listing — one that was probably going to convert better anyway.
